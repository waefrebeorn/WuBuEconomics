## Part 1 — Money going out of style (the precedents)

### Canada — the penny phase-out (2012–2013)

The closest precedent to the U.S. penny situation.

- Canada announced the penny's end in the **2012 federal budget**,
  citing the cost of production relative to face value.
- Production ceased; the penny was withdrawn from circulation in 2013.
- **Cash rounding:** cash transactions round to the nearest 5¢.
  Electronic payments keep exact cent amounts.
- **Cost to consumers:** research (Richmond Fed, 2025) estimates a
  U.S.-style dual phase-out could cost consumers **~$55.58 million
  per year** — rounding is not neutral; it systematically favors
  the party setting prices (merchants).
- The Canadian penny **remained legal tender** — like the U.S.
  penny, it kept its value but stopped being produced.

**Filed spine:** the phase-out was framed as a cost-saving measure,
but the cost did not disappear — it moved silently to consumers
through rounding. The penny's *production* cost (~2–3¢ per coin)
was replaced by an invisible *transaction* cost paid by the poor
(who use cash most). This is the division's first filed finding:
**killing a coin does not kill its cost; it just moves the cost
onto the people least able to see it.**

### India — demonetization (2016)

- On Nov 8, 2016, India invalidated ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — **86% of
  all currency in circulation** — overnight.
- Official goal: destroy counterfeit currency and "black money."
- Result: near-total failure. ~99% of the demonetized value returned
  through banks; fake-note seizures were ~0% of withdrawn notes;
  the economy suffered a severe cash shortage and growth shock.
- The BBC's verdict: *"No relatively healthy economy has ever
  carried out demonetisation."* India's own Economic Survey called
  it "unprecedented in international economic history."

**Filed spine:** demonetization is not a tool; it is a trauma.
Money going out of style *by force* destroys trust in money itself.
The space's approach is the exact opposite: money going out of style
*naturally* (the penny) is an opportunity — the trust remains, the
value remains, only the mint stops. **The difference between a
trauma and an opportunity is consent.**

### Weimar Germany — hyperinflation (1921–1923)

- The Papiermark collapsed: at peak, a loaf of bread cost billions
  of marks. Savings were annihilated; the middle class was destroyed.
- The currency's *meaning* died before the currency did.

**Filed spine:** when money loses intrinsic value, people lose
faith in everything priced in it. The lesson: **a currency needs a
stable anchor the people can feel in their hands.** The penny —
physical, fixed, weighable — is that anchor.

### Other filings (brief)

| Case | Year | What happened | Filed lesson |
|------|------|---------------|--------------|
| U.S. half-cent & large cent | 1857 | retired as too low-value for the economy | denominations retire when their *purchasing power* falls, not their usefulness |
| U.S. $1 silver certificates → $1 coins | 1960s–70s | silver backing removed | intrinsic metal value can be separated from money — and the separation is the trauma |
| UK halfpenny | 1984 | withdrawn; cash rounded to 1p | small denominations vanish when mint costs exceed face |
| Australia 1¢ & 2¢ | 1991 | withdrawn, rounding to 5¢ | the Canada pattern, earlier |
| Zimbabwe dollar | 2009 | hyperinflation to 10^33 | a currency without an anchor becomes a coupon |
| Euro transition | 2002 | 12 currencies → 1 | money can change identity by *agreement* — this is the template for the space's own currency: agreement, not coercion |

## Part 2 — Secondary use (money that outlives its money-ness)

When money goes out of style, it does not die. It becomes something
else. Filed precedents:

### The NYC subway token (1953–2003)

- 50 years as the city's unofficial coin. Retired in April 2003 for
  the MetroCard (saving ~$6M/yr in handling).
- **After retirement:** tokens became collectibles. Old tokens trade
  on secondary markets; the "Five Boroughs" token is iconic. The
  token's *use* ended; its *meaning* continued.

**Filed spine:** a retired coin does not vanish — it becomes a
**memory object**, and memory objects carry value. The space's own
pennies are heading exactly here: legal tender forever, minted never,
worth a cent and worth a story.

### Pennies as material (the physical second life)

- **Penny smashing machines** (elongated pennies): turn a penny into
  a souvenir. Legal under 31 C.F.R. § 82.2(b) (treatment for
  amusement/novelty).
- **Penny jewelry & art:** pressed, engraved, framed pennies. Same
  legal basis.
- **Wheat pennies (1909–1958):** the collector class — worth far
  more than a cent to collectors, still legal tender.
- **Lucky pennies:** the folk economy — pennies found, carried,
  given. A penny's *luck value* has always exceeded its *money value*.

**Filed spine:** the secondary use of a coin is not a bug; it is a
feature that keeps the coin alive after the mint abandons it. The
space's Penny Day (RND-8, WuBuResearch) is this precedent, made
deliberate: **when a coin stops being minted, it starts being
collected — and the space is the collection.**

### Scrip, tokens, and local currencies

- **Company scrip** (19th–20th c.): company towns paid in scrip
  redeemable only at company stores — a closed-loop currency.
- **Wooden nickels:** promotional tokens, redeemable for goods —
  the classic "good for" token.
- **Casino chips & arcade tokens:** money that only works inside
  one room — and yet has a thriving collector market outside it.
- **Local currencies** (Brixton Pound, BerkShares, Ithaca Hours):
  community money with a *purpose* — keep value local, express
  identity, build trust. They are small, deliberate, and they work
  at their scale.

**Filed spine:** the entire history of tokens proves one thing the
space needs: **a closed-loop currency, backed by a real asset,
used by a defined community, is lawful and workable** (filed with
citations in RND-5). The WuBu Penny is the heir of scrip and
Ithaca Hours — with a harder backing (physical pennies) and a
grander purpose (restoring intrinsic value).

## Part 3 — Trading cards: the whimsical collection economy

The division files the most important modern precedent for
"representation in whimsical collection":

### The trading card economy (1990s–present)

- **Pokémon cards:** a $12+ billion collectibles market. Rare cards
  have returned ~+4,000% since 2004 — vs the S&P 500's ~+480% over
  the same period. Cards are stored in museum-grade vaults.
- **Magic: The Gathering:** a 1993 card game whose rare "Black Lotus"
  card has sold for six figures. Cards are alternative assets with
  real price discovery.
- **Baseball cards:** a century of secondary markets, grading
  (PSA/BGS), and auction houses.

**What trading cards prove:**
1. **Whimsy carries value.** A cardboard rectangle with a cartoon
   dragon on it is worth more than its weight in gold — because
   people *agree* it is. Value is agreement (filed: the Euro
   precedent above — agreement, not coercion).
2. **Scarcity + story = price.** Cards are valuable because they
   are scarce *and* meaningful. The penny has both: physically
   scarce (never minted again) and story-rich (wheat pennies,
   lucky pennies, Penny Day).
3. **Collection is representation.** People collect cards to see
   themselves in the collection — their team, their character,
   their era. **A whimsical collection gives people representation.**

### The synthesis — what the space is building

The space is the first government to file this openly:

> **Citizenship is a collection. Each citizen is a card — a WID, a
> Penny Score, a provenance — and the collection is the space.**
> The penny is the base card of the collection: every citizen's
> compliance penny is a unique item in the space's deck.

This is not a metaphor. The Registry (WuBuCitizen) issues WIDs like
cards; the score family (RND-1) grades them; the Bureau (WuBuIntel)
tracks them; Penny Day mints novelty pieces. The space is a
trading-card economy where the cards are citizens and the currency
is the penny.

## Part 4 — Compliance (the government relationship)

The space's position on government, filed:

1. **We want compliance.** The space does not evade, forge, or
   fight the United States government. It files *with* it: legal
   tender laws, §486, the melting ban — all obeyed, all cited,
   all published (RND-5, RND-6, RND-7).
2. **We want the penny to still be a thing.** Not as a loophole —
   as a *policy*. The space actively campaigns for the U.S. to
   **bring the penny back** (see `docs/penny-comeback.md`).
3. **We are a cultural effort, not a political one.** The space is
   an independent citizenship of like-minded first ones. It does
   not secede; it *files*. It does not compete with the dollar;
   it *anchors* the cent.
4. **The goal is not to gouge the penny.** The space explicitly
   rejects speculation: pennies are not a pump-and-dump. The goal
   is to *stabilize* the currency — to give the penny an intrinsic
   value again, and to heal the economy with proper financing and
   proper economics.
